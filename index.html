Part 1: from the opening argument

Found it, can’t be found, or still looking

In the skit
Two Dogmatists fight over whose packet is better. The Academic says nobody can know. The Pyrrhonist, in a posh accent, suspends judgement and keeps eating.

The short version. Sextus says every search ends in one of three ways. You think you’ve found the answer, you declare it can’t be found, or you keep looking. Only the third is the skeptic.

The three stances, as Sextus lays them out (PH 1.1–4)
StanceWhat they claimSextus’s examplesIn our skit
Dogmatist“I have found the truth.”Aristotle, Epicurus, the StoicsThe two hawkers: “Mine is the best!”
Academic Skeptic“The truth cannot be found.”Clitomachus, Carneades“Nobody can ever know which one is best!”
Pyrrhonian Skeptic“I’m still looking.”The Pyrrhonists“I suspend judgement.”
Why the Academic is a dogmatist pointing the other wayNegative dogmatism

The Academic sounds humble, but “knowledge is impossible” is itself a confident claim about knowledge. To know that nobody can know which chicken rice is best, you’d need to know a great deal about the limits of taste, evidence and human minds. So Sextus treats it as another form of dogmatism. Modern scholars call it negative dogmatism, though Bett notes this isn’t a term Sextus himself uses (Bett, p. 404).

For the curious. Whether the historical Academics really held this firm view is disputed. Bett says Sextus links it to the Academics “rightly or wrongly” (p. 404), and some ancient sources suggest they too avoided committing to anything. Sextus’s picture is partly a contrast that helps define the Pyrrhonist (PH 1.220–235).

What a Pyrrhonist actually isAn ability, not a doctrine

The word skeptikos means “inquirer”: someone who keeps examining (Bett, p. 404). The Pyrrhonists also called themselves searchers, suspenders and the puzzled (PH 1.7). Sextus’s definition is one of the most important sentences he wrote:

The skeptical ability is one that produces oppositions among things that appear and things that are thought in any way whatsoever, one from which, because of the equal strength in the opposing objects and accounts, we come first to suspension of judgement, and after that to tranquility.
PH 1.8, as quoted by Bett, p. 404
Four terms from that sentence
GreekMeaningChicken rice version
dunamisAn ability: a way of doing things, not a theoryA way of handling chicken rice debates, not a rival opinion about chicken rice
isostheneiaEqual strength: both sides seem equally convincingStall A’s chicken is juicier, Stall B’s rice is more fragrant. Neither wins outright.
epochéSuspension of judgement: the mind neither affirms nor denies (PH 1.10)“I won’t say which is best.”
ataraxiaTranquillity: freedom from mental disturbanceNo more lying awake defending your stall
The Pyrrhonist doesn’t doubt the tasteAppearances vs how things really are

A common misunderstanding: the Pyrrhonist does not deny that things appear as they do. Sextus’s own example is honey (PH 1.19–20). He accepts that honey appears sweet to him. What he investigates is whether honey is sweet by its nature.

So in the skit, the Pyrrhonist can happily say the chicken appears succulent. That’s a report of experience, and he goes along with it. What he suspends judgement on is the further claim that it is the best by nature.

Sextus also says the skeptic accepts feelings forced on him: when he’s hot, he won’t insist he isn’t hot. What he avoids is assent to non-evident matters, the hidden truths that philosophers and scientists argue about (PH 1.13).

For the curious. How much does the skeptic believe? Does he suspend only on deep theories and keep everyday beliefs (“there is a table in front of me”), or does he suspend on those too, keeping only the bare feel of his experience? Bett notes that Sextus’s “on the side of ordinary life” stance suggests the first, but that this isn’t consistently sustained (Bett, p. 406). Scholars call this the debate between “urbane” and “rustic” readings.

Part 2: from the 1-to-10 poll

Is this chicken rice even real?

In the skit
We asked how sure you are that the chicken rice exists, then took a very small bite to “test” it.

The short version. Bostrom doesn’t claim we’re in a simulation. He argues that at least one of three possibilities must be true, and one of them is that we are. Tasting the rice can’t settle it, because the taste could be simulated too.

Bostrom’s argument, step by stepWhy you can’t comfortably reject all three

Nick Bostrom (2003, “Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?”) argues that at least one of these is very likely true:

  1. Almost all civilisations at our stage die out before they become technologically mature, meaning able to run detailed simulations of minds.
  2. Almost no technologically mature civilisations are interested in running such simulations, for example of their own ancestors.
  3. We are almost certainly living in a simulation.

Suppose you reject (1) and (2). Then many civilisations survive, and many of them run huge numbers of simulations, so simulated minds vastly outnumber real ones. If you can’t tell which kind of mind you are, Bostrom says you should spread your confidence evenly across all minds with experiences like yours (his “bland indifference principle”). On those numbers you’re probably simulated. So rejecting (3) commits you to (1) or (2).

What makes something a skeptical hypothesisDreams, demons, vats and simulations

A skeptical hypothesis is a scenario that would make many of your everyday beliefs false and would feel exactly like normal life from the inside. Famous examples:

  • Descartes’s dream (Meditations, 1641): am I sure I’m not dreaming this hawker centre?
  • Descartes’s evil demon: a powerful deceiver could be feeding me false experiences.
  • The brain in a vat: a scientist stimulates a disembodied brain.
  • Bostrom’s simulation: the computing version.

Why the bite didn’t help: every test you could run, whether tasting, smelling or asking the uncle, happens inside the scenario you’re trying to rule out. Simulated chicken rice would pass every test.

A historical twistThe ancients didn’t doubt the whole world

As far as we can tell, the ancient Pyrrhonists didn’t use whole-world scenarios like these. Doubting the external world as a whole is a mostly modern, Cartesian worry; the classicist Myles Burnyeat famously argued this. Ancient skeptics worked case by case: does this honey really taste sweet, is this tower really round? Bett also stresses that Pyrrhonism “differs in multiple ways from Cartesian skepticism” (p. 403), which is where Part 3 picks up.

Part 3: from “got use fresh chicken or not?”

Everyday doubt, deep doubt, and two pictures of one plate

In the skit
An argument about frozen chicken, a sudden lecture on protein percentages, and a demand to “give me a reason”.

The short version. Ordinary doubt checks one claim and can be settled. Philosophical doubt questions the basis of knowledge itself. Descartes uses it to reach certainty; the Pyrrhonist lives with it.

Ordinary doubtLocal, reasoned, settleable
  • It’s local: it targets one specific claim.
  • It has a reason: “the colour doesn’t look right.”
  • It can be settled: ask the stall owner, check the reviews, taste it.

“Is your chicken fresh or frozen?” is ordinary doubt. It works entirely within everyday life.

The manifest image and the scientific imageSellars’s two pictures of the world

Wilfrid Sellars (“Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man”, 1962) described two ways of picturing the world.

Manifest imageScientific image
What it isThe world as we ordinarily experience it: people, objects, colours, tastesThe world as the sciences describe it: molecules, proteins, cells, fields
Chicken rice“Juicy, fragrant, a bit spicy”“About 20–24% protein, small amounts of lipid…”
Who uses itEveryone, every dayScientists, in theory

Both describe the same plate in completely different terms. How they fit together is one of philosophy’s big questions. Sellars himself leaned towards the scientific image on what really exists (“science is the measure of all things”), while holding that the manifest image is indispensable for people and their purposes.

Cartesian doubt vs Pyrrhonian doubtA threat to defeat, or an outlook to live with

Bett puts the contrast sharply: for Descartes, skepticism is “a threat to be warded off”; for the Pyrrhonists, “an outlook to be embraced” (p. 403).

Cartesian doubtPyrrhonian doubt
SourceRené Descartes, Meditations (1641)Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE), as developed by Aenesidemus and recorded by Sextus
AttitudeA tool to get rid of uncertaintyA way of life
MethodDoubt everything that could be false, to find something certainSet opposing arguments against each other
GoalCertainty (“I think, therefore I am”)Tranquillity (ataraxia)
Chicken rice“Give me a reason! Prove it, or I reject it.”“Today buy from her, tomorrow buy from him.”

In short: Descartes doubts in order to stop doubting. The Pyrrhonist doubts and finds he no longer needs to stop.

For the curious. Bett adds that Pyrrhonism isn’t specially about epistemology at all. Sextus applies the same method to physics, ethics, grammar and astrology. In Bett’s words, it “is about epistemology; it is not a position within epistemology” (p. 407).

Part 4: from “when they feel hungry, they just eat”

How does a skeptic get out of bed?

In the skit
The Pyrrhonist doesn’t need to judge whether chicken rice is objectively good. Hungry? Eat.

The short version. The skeptic acts on how things seem, not on beliefs about how they really are. Hunger, habit, custom and skill are enough to get through the day.

The objection: a life without beliefs is impossibleThe ancient apraxia charge

If the Pyrrhonist suspends judgement on everything, how does he decide anything? This “inaction” (apraxia) objection was “standardly leveled against skeptics in antiquity” (Bett, p. 406). Later anecdotes even joked that Pyrrho had to be saved by friends from carts and cliffs, though these are probably caricatures.

Sextus’s answer: the fourfold guide to lifePH 1.21–24

It’s quite possible, Sextus replies, to act in light of how things strike you without taking a stand on whether they really are that way (Bett, p. 406). His guide has four parts:

  1. Guidance of nature: we can naturally perceive and think.
  2. The pressure of feelings: hunger leads us to food, thirst to drink.
  3. Laws and customs: we follow the practices of our society.
  4. Teaching of skills: we practise the crafts we’ve been taught.

So the hungry Pyrrhonist walks to the hawker centre and eats. No theory of which chicken rice is objectively best, or of what flavour “really” is, is needed.

A later challenge: Hume“Nature is always too strong for principle”

David Hume thought radical skepticism couldn’t survive real life: “nature is always too strong for principle” (Enquiry, 1748, Section 12). Leave the study, get hungry, and your doubts vanish. A Pyrrhonist might reply that Hume is agreeing with him: that’s exactly why the skeptic follows nature and appearances rather than theory.

Part 5: the skeptic’s toolkit

The Modes: ready-made ways to balance an argument

Where these come from
The Ten Modes are attributed to Aenesidemus (1st century BCE), the Five to the otherwise unknown Agrippa (Bett, p. 409).

The short version. The Modes are standard argument patterns for producing “equal strength”. The Ten show that the same thing appears differently in different conditions. The Five work against any claim at all.

The Ten Modes, on a plate

Tap a card to flip it and see the chicken rice version.

Sextus says all ten come down to relativity in the end (PH 1.39).

The Five ModesThe general-purpose set (PH 1.165–169)

In the order Sextus introduces them (Bett, p. 408):

  1. Undecidable dispute: people disagree, and the disagreement can’t be settled.
  2. Infinite regress: every reason needs a further reason, forever.
  3. Relativity: things appear only relative to someone or something.
  4. Hypothesis: you stop the chain by simply assuming something.
  5. Circularity: your reason relies on the very thing you’re trying to prove.

The Ten and the Five are two separate sets. Sextus says the Ten are older, so the Five must have been developed in the two centuries or more between Aenesidemus and Sextus himself (Bett, p. 409). Bett also notes that Relativity “sits awkwardly” among the Five; the other four fit together much better (p. 408).

Part 6: from the audience “why?” chain

The “why?” trap

In the skit
We kept asking you why, until someone slapped the table: “Because I say so!”

The short version. Keep asking “why?” and any defence of a claim ends in one of three places: reasons forever, reasons in a circle, or a reason you just assume. Today this is called Agrippa’s trilemma.

Try it yourself

Defend the claim. Each time you’re asked why, give the next reason.

    The three exits, and why each one failsPH 1.171–174
    ExitWhat happensChicken rice versionWhy it fails
    Infinite regressReasons go on forever“Because the chilli is balanced.” “Why?” “Because…”The justification is never finished
    CircularityThe reason assumes the conclusion“It’s the best because it tastes best.”You’ve assumed what you were proving
    Hypothesis (“the parent slam”)You just stop“Because I say so!” (slaps table)Sextus treats this as arbitrary: you could just as well have assumed something else

    Bett points out this is only the beginning of an argument. Many would deny these are the only three options. Aristotle, for example, would dispute that starting-points which need no further justification are merely arbitrary “hypotheses” (Bett, p. 408). The name “Agrippan trilemma” is modern and has become widespread in recent epistemology (p. 411).

    What the trilemma does not showNot “truth is impossible”

    It’s tempting to say the trilemma proves “truth is impossible” or “nothing can be justified”, which is exactly what the Academic in our skit would say. A Pyrrhonist shouldn’t, because that is negative dogmatism again. He only reports that so far, every attempt seems to hit one of these walls, so he suspends judgement and keeps going. (Whether Sextus himself always sticks to this is one of Bett’s criticisms; see Part 9.)

    Link to the course: the Prover–Skeptic gameDutilh Novaes (2020)

    In Catarina Dutilh Novaes’s account of deduction, reasoning is a dialogue between a Prover, who tries to convince, and a Skeptic, who checks every step. One of the Skeptic’s moves is simply to ask “why does this follow?” (The Dialogical Roots of Deduction, pp. 52–53).

    The trilemma shows what happens if that “why?” never stops: the Prover runs out of good moves. Dutilh Novaes partly handles this by treating a Skeptic who questions every step, like Lewis Carroll’s Tortoise, as misusing the game. Workable games need some agreed moves that aren’t questioned (pp. 58–59).

    Where justification stops: Wittgenstein and SellarsShared practice and the space of reasons

    Ludwig Wittgenstein (Philosophical Investigations, 1953) argued that justification bottoms out in shared practice:

    • Following a rule is a practice, not something one person can do privately (§202).
    • When reasons run out, “I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned”, and I can only say “this is simply what I do” (§217).
    • Our agreement is ultimately agreement in a form of life (§241).

    The phrase “the space of reasons” comes from Sellars (“Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind”, 1956, §36). It names the practice of giving and asking for reasons.

    Applied to chicken rice: we can only argue about which stall is better because we already share standards. The rice shouldn’t be mushy, the chicken shouldn’t be dry, and the chilli shouldn’t taste like strawberries. In the skit, people picked different stalls but gave the same kinds of reasons.

    For the curious. Robert Fogelin, who calls himself a neo-Pyrrhonist, sees a Pyrrhonist strand in Wittgenstein: philosophy as therapy, and philosophy as needing to come to an end. Bett notes that others question how useful this comparison really is (Bett, p. 412).

    Part 7: going further

    Will the stall be good tomorrow?

    Connection
    Hume’s “custom and habit” is a real-life version of the trilemma’s third exit.

    The short version. We trust that tomorrow’s chicken rice will be like today’s, but the only support for that trust is past experience, which is exactly what’s in question. Hume says we rely on habit, not proof.

    The queue dialogueHume’s problem of induction

    A: I’m queueing again tomorrow. This stall will be great.
    B: Why?
    A: Because it’s been great every day for five years.
    B: Why does the past tell you about tomorrow?
    A: Because nature is uniform. Tomorrow will resemble today.
    B: Why believe nature is uniform?
    A: Because it always has been, every time we’ve checked.
    B: But that’s the past again. Why trust it for the future?

    This is Hume’s problem of induction (Enquiry, Sections 4–5). Every argument from past to future relies on the assumption that the future will resemble the past, but that assumption can only be supported by past experience, so the reasoning goes in a circle. Hume concluded we rely on custom or habit: “Custom, then, is the great guide of human life.”

    That’s one of the trilemma’s exits in real life: we stop at an assumption we can’t justify, and live by it anyway, much like the Pyrrhonist’s reliance on habit and custom (Part 4).

    Part 8: from the cold box of rice

    Why suspend judgement at all?

    In the skit
    One character spent 45 minutes building an airtight proof. His rice went cold. The other just ate.

    The short version. The skeptics went looking for truth to find peace of mind. They never found it, suspended judgement, and the peace arrived anyway.

    Opposing arguments› Equal strength› Suspension of judgement› Tranquillity
    The origin story, and Apelles’ spongePH 1.12, 1.26–29

    Sextus says the skeptic starts out as a normal philosopher, seeking tranquillity by discovering the truth. The attempt fails: every position meets an equally strong opposite. Unable to decide, he suspends judgement, and that very suspension brings the tranquillity he was hoping for (PH 1.12, 26; Bett, p. 405). It follows “as a shadow follows a body” (PH 1.29).

    Sextus compares this to the painter Apelles, who couldn’t paint the foam on a horse’s mouth. In frustration he threw his sponge at the painting, and the mark it left looked exactly like foam (PH 1.28).

    Chicken rice version: the ancient skeptics went searching for the best chicken rice to finally have peace of mind. They never found it. When they stopped needing to know, the peace came anyway, and the rice was still warm.

    The goal: tranquillity and moderate feelingPH 1.25–30
    • Tranquillity (ataraxia) in matters of opinion: you’re no longer troubled about whether your stall is really the best.
    • Moderate feeling (metriopatheia) in what we can’t avoid: you still feel hunger, but without the extra torment of believing hunger is objectively terrible.

    Bett observes that whenever Sextus explains why suspension brings peace, the focus is on dropping beliefs about what is really good or bad. Believe something is really good and you care desperately about getting or keeping it; shed that belief and “nothing matters to anything like the same extent” (Bett, p. 405).

    Tranquillity and the good lifeAtaraxia and eudaimonia

    Most ancient Greek philosophy asks how to live a flourishing life (eudaimonia). The Pyrrhonist answer is that the main obstacle is the anxiety of holding firm opinions: defending them, fearing they’re wrong, needing to be right. Remove the demand for certainty and you remove that anxiety. As Bett puts it, Pyrrhonism is “a way of life, one that its adherents actively recommend” (p. 406).

    And it is a skill, not a thesis. The Pyrrhonist doesn’t hold that nothing can be known; that would be negative dogmatism. The peace comes not from solving the question but from dropping it.

    Part 9: going further, with our reading

    Does Pyrrhonism live up to its own rules?

    Our reading
    Richard Bett, “Pyrrhonian Skepticism”, in The Routledge Companion to Epistemology (2011), pp. 403–413.

    The short version. Bett argues that Sextus doesn’t always practise what he preaches. The skeptic claims to be an open-minded inquirer with no definite positions, yet his methods sometimes look like a fixed routine, and his Modes sometimes read like arguments that we must suspend judgement.

    Inquirer, or tranquillity-seeker?Bett, pp. 404–405

    The skeptic is defined as someone still searching. But Sextus’s definition (PH 1.8) describes something else: a practised ability to assemble opposing arguments so that both sides feel equally strong. That skeptic looks less like an open-minded inquirer, for whom finding the truth is still possible, and more like someone who has decided tranquillity is what he wants and that suspension is the surest way to it.

    Bett allows a way out. “Equal strength” can be read psychologically: you simply find both sides equally persuasive. Suspension can be something that happens to you rather than something you decide, and wanting tranquillity can be a brute fact about the skeptic’s motivation. On that reading Sextus doesn’t fall back into dogmatism. But Bett still sees a “pre-arranged system of skeptical activity” that doesn’t fit the picture of someone still searching (p. 405).

    Chicken rice version. Is the Pyrrhonist really still tasting every stall with an open mind? Or has he just learned a reliable trick for never having to pick one?

    Why should suspension bring peace?Bett, p. 405

    Whenever Sextus explains why suspension brings tranquillity, he points to dropping beliefs about what is really good or bad. That raises three questions:

    1. If the trouble comes from beliefs about good and bad, why suspend judgement on every topic?
    2. If such beliefs are troubling because of what they’re about, whether or not they’re true, why does the failed search for truth matter at all?
    3. In the origin story, equal strength is something you simply stumble on. But the settled skeptic deliberately builds balanced oppositions. Bett notes this “deliberate artifice” could invite charges of intellectual dishonesty, though the skeptic would reply that such charges assume standards of rationality he doesn’t sign up to.
    Do the Modes turn into dogma?Bett, pp. 407–410

    This is Bett’s sharpest point. Taken by themselves, the Modes are “most easily read as arguments to the effect that we must suspend judgment” (p. 407), and that “must” sounds logical, not just psychological.

    • The Five Modes look like a knock-down argument that rational justification is impossible. Sextus concludes it is “absolutely necessary to suspend judgment” (PH 1.177; Bett, p. 409). That makes them a position within epistemology, which is not what Sextus usually means by skepticism.
    • The Ten Modes often set up oppositions you can’t feel at the same time. You can’t taste chicken rice as your cat does, or as someone else does, and you can’t be sick and healthy at once. So Sextus can’t be saying “feel the equal pull of these and find yourself suspending”. He has to be saying “there’s no way to decide, so you have no choice”, which is the force of argument again (p. 409).
    • Several of the Ten end by saying we cannot say how things are by nature. That depends on a particular view of what a thing’s nature would be and how we’d grasp it, which by Sextus’s own standards is a dogmatic conclusion (pp. 409–410).

    Bett’s explanation is historical. These relativity-based conclusions may be traces of an earlier form of Pyrrhonism, linked to Aenesidemus, which did make claims like “nothing is good or bad by nature” (pp. 409–410).

    Chicken rice version. “Taste depends on who’s eating, so we cannot know which stall is best” sounds a lot like the Academic in our skit. The posh Pyrrhonist has borrowed the Academic’s line.

    Can the Modes be rescued?Williams’s proposal, and Sextus’s own replies

    Michael Williams suggests the Modes are only one side of an opposition, with the other side being the confident theories of knowledge held by non-skeptics. Then the Modes would just be the normal Pyrrhonist method applied to epistemology. Bett doesn’t think this fits Sextus’s text, which presents the Modes by themselves as producing suspension. But he agrees it would be a better, more consistent use of them, and that Sextus does use them this way elsewhere (Bett, pp. 410–411).

    Sextus also has general replies. He says the skeptic reports how things appear to him, like a chronicler, without asserting it (PH 1.4). His arguments work like a purgative drug that flushes itself out along with everything else (PH 1.206), or a ladder you kick away after climbing it (Against the Logicians 2.481). Whether these replies work is still debated.

    How far does suspension reach?Bett, p. 406

    Does the skeptic suspend only on deep theories, keeping everyday beliefs like “I’m sitting in my office”, or on those too? Sextus’s claim to be “on the side of ordinary life” suggests the first, but Bett says this “is not clear” it is consistently sustained. Everyday beliefs might commit you to deeper ones, such as that there is a criterion of truth, which Sextus also questions. How you answer affects how believable the skeptical life is (p. 406).

    Part 10: from the replay

    So what? Pyrrhonism for students

    In the skit
    The opening argument plays again. This time, the two hawkers swap a spoonful each.

    The short version. You can care, act and enjoy things without demanding certainty about them. That makes you harder to drag into online outrage, and a better reader of theories.

    In everyday lifeEcho chambers and binary positions

    Algorithms, echo chambers and outrage cycles reward rigid, binary positions: Stall A or Stall B, us or them. Defending those positions all the time costs a lot of emotional energy. A Pyrrhonian attitude still lives by appearances and acts sensibly, still cares about other people and takes part in daily life, but drops the demand for certainty on matters that can’t be settled.

    That isn’t indifference. It’s giving up intellectual point-scoring in exchange for peace of mind.

    In our studiesReading theories without becoming their fan club

    Every week we meet persuasive theories. It’s easy to fall for the first clever one and defend it dogmatically, and just as easy to swing to cynical nihilism when it gets taken apart. A Pyrrhonian student asks instead:

    • What are the strongest opposing arguments to this theory?
    • Where does its chain of justification hit an Agrippan wall?

    That lets you appreciate a model’s beauty and usefulness without mistaking it for reality, much like our seminar discussions of scientific realism and anti-realism.

    Eat the chicken rice while it’s still warm.

    Taste it, enjoy it, compare it, keep inquiring. Just don’t let the need to prove it’s the best ruin your lunch.
